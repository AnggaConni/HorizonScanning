How the environment
shapes human behaviour

We often assume that behaviour is shaped mainly by what people are explicitly taught. But what they repeatedly see, hear, imitate, and come to regard as normal can also influence how they think and respond.

✳
The central idea
A child does not need to be directly taught a swear word to start using it. Repeated exposure, observing peers, imitation, and group norms can all contribute to learned behaviour. These influences are powerful, but they are not automatic: people differ, and context matters.

01 · The central question

“I never taught my child to speak like that. Where did they learn it?”

One possible answer is the social environment. People learn not only through direct instruction, but also by observing others and noticing which behaviours are repeated, rewarded, tolerated, or socially accepted.

Learning does not always look like a lesson

Peers, siblings, adults, admired figures, and media characters can all become behavioural models. A child may pick up a phrase, tone of voice, gesture, or way of handling conflict without anyone formally teaching it.

If a behaviour is frequently visible, attracts attention, or appears to be accepted by a group, it may become easier to imitate. If the child sees that the behaviour leads to rejection or other negative consequences, imitation may be less likely.

An important distinction: direct teaching is only one route to learning. Everyday surroundings provide examples, cues, and social feedback—often repeatedly and without deliberate instruction.
Key nuance

Influence is not destiny

Not every exposed child will copy a behaviour. Temperament, age, relationships, parental guidance, personal values, and the consequences of the behaviour all matter.

The point is not that children should be isolated from anyone who behaves differently. It is that adults should pay attention to the environments in which children spend time—and help them think critically about what they observe.

02 · The psychological mechanisms

Several concepts work together

“Priming” is relevant, but it does not explain every kind of imitation. The examples below involve overlapping mechanisms that should be distinguished rather than treated as one single effect.

1

Priming

Exposure to one stimulus can influence how quickly or readily related information is processed later. A recently activated word, idea, or association may become more accessible.

In plain English: something you encountered earlier can become easier to bring to mind later. Priming can influence accessibility without necessarily causing a person to imitate anyone.
2

Observational learning

People can learn by watching what others do and what happens to them. They may acquire a new response without receiving a direct instruction themselves.

In plain English: “I saw someone do it, so I learned that this is something people do.” This is central to Albert Bandura’s social learning work.
3

Mimicry & social contagion

Mimicry is the tendency to copy aspects of another person’s behaviour. Social contagion describes how behaviours, emotions, or practices can spread through social contact.

In plain English: a behaviour can spread from person to person, sometimes without a deliberate decision to copy it.
4

Conformity

People may adjust their behaviour to match a group, because they want to fit in or because they assume the group knows what is appropriate.

In plain English: “Everyone else is doing it, so perhaps this is what I am supposed to do.”
5

Normalisation & descriptive norms

Repeated behaviour can begin to feel ordinary. Descriptive norms are perceptions about what people commonly do—not necessarily what they approve of or what is morally right.

In plain English: “This happens all the time around here, so it must be normal.”
6

Internalisation

A group’s expectations or practices may eventually become part of a person’s own habits, assumptions, or values. This is not inevitable, but it can happen over time.

In plain English: a behaviour that began as something copied from others may become something the person does automatically or personally endorses.
Do not collapse the concepts: priming concerns accessibility and processing; observational learning concerns learning by watching; conformity concerns adjusting to group influence; normalisation concerns what comes to feel ordinary. They can interact, but they are not interchangeable.

04 · Two case studies from everyday life

Two examples—and why they may happen

The first example is mainly about word accessibility and semantic association. The second is more directly about social learning, imitation, and peer norms.

Case study 1 · Adult language and priming

Someone repeatedly hears “disgusting” and later reaches for related language when frustrated

Scenario: An adult repeatedly encounters words such as disgusting, nasty, or terrible. Later, when frustrated while describing a piece of work, they might say “a disgusting project,” “nasty evidence,” or choose a related negative expression more readily than they otherwise would.

1. Repeated exposureThe words and their emotional associations are encountered.
2. ActivationRelated concepts become more accessible in memory.
3. FrustrationThe person needs language to label or express a negative evaluation.
4. Word selectionAn accessible word or related expression may come to mind more readily.

Why might this happen?

Words are connected in semantic memory. “Disgusting,” “nasty,” and “awful” share negative meaning and emotional associations. Activating one part of this network can sometimes make related concepts easier to retrieve.

When a person is frustrated, they may have less patience for carefully searching for a precise phrase. A readily accessible expression can win the competition for selection.

What is the psychological explanation?

This is a plausible illustration of semantic priming and lexical accessibility. The prior exposure may influence which words are readily available during later language production.

However, the exact phrase is not guaranteed. “Disgusting” does not mechanically cause someone to say “nasty evidence.” Mood, topic, personal vocabulary, context, and individual differences also shape word choice.

Key distinction: this example does not require copying another person’s behaviour. The mechanism is about how prior exposure can make related words or concepts more accessible—not necessarily about imitation or adopting a social norm.

What this case illustrates: the mind does not retrieve every possible word with equal ease. Recent or repeated activation can sometimes tilt what comes to mind first.

Case study 2 · A child, peer language, and observational learning

A child hears friends use swear words—and later uses one when angry

Scenario: A child regularly hears peers use expressions such as “anjing,” “babi,” or other swear words. The parents do not use those words at home and never deliberately teach them. One day, when the child becomes frustrated, the child blurts out one of the expressions.

1. ExposureThe child repeatedly hears peers use the words in real situations.
2. ObservationThe child notices who says them, when, and how others react.
3. Learning & rehearsalThe words become familiar and may be tried or repeated.
4. Retrieval under emotionDuring anger, a familiar expression may be used as an available response.

Why might the child copy it?

Observational learning: children learn by watching peers, not only by following parental instructions. Friends can become influential models because they are present often and matter socially.

Repetition: hearing the same words repeatedly makes them familiar. The child learns the sound, context, and social use of the expression.

Social feedback: if peers laugh, pay attention, or treat the language as ordinary, the child may infer that it is acceptable in that group. Attention can unintentionally reinforce repetition.

Why might it appear during frustration?

The child may have learned that the expression is used in moments of anger, excitement, teasing, or emphasis. When a similar emotional situation occurs, that learned phrase may be retrieved as a ready-made response.

Priming and accessibility may contribute, but observational learning and social reinforcement are the more direct explanations for how the child acquired the expression in the first place.

In other words, the child may not be copying a specific friend at that exact moment. The child may be using language already learned from repeated social exposure.

Why parents can be surprised: they may focus on what they personally taught, while overlooking the many hours of peer interaction in which children observe, practise, and receive feedback about language.
Important caution: one swear word does not prove that a child has been negatively influenced or that a particular friend caused it. Children also experiment with language, test boundaries, and respond to media or other settings. Look for repeated patterns and context rather than blaming one child.

What this case illustrates: the child may acquire a behaviour without direct instruction. The social environment can act as an informal teacher.

05 · A group-behaviour demonstration

The waiting-room conformity video

You also described a video in which people in a waiting room follow a group behaviour—then continue even after the original group members leave. The clip below is commonly shared as a demonstration of social conformity.

Brain Games · Social conformity demonstrationOpen video on YouTube ↗

What does the demonstration suggest?

  1. A newcomer sees several people behaving in the same unusual way.
  2. They may assume there is a reason or an unwritten rule.
  3. They begin to copy the group, even without knowing why.
  4. After the original participants leave, the newcomer may continue the behaviour.
  5. A later newcomer sees the behaviour already established and copies it in turn.
Evidence note: treat this as a popular demonstration, not a controlled research paper. I could not verify that this particular video originated in Japan, so this page does not attribute it to Japan.

Why might the behaviour continue after the original group leaves?

The behaviour may have become a local norm. The person no longer needs the original actors to be present: the setting itself now contains a visible pattern that seems to signal “this is what people do here.” A newcomer can then learn the practice from the person who adopted it earlier.

This resembles conformity and can be discussed alongside an information cascade: people use the actions of those before them as information when they are uncertain. But the video alone cannot establish exactly which psychological mechanism caused each participant to act.

07 · The wider lesson

The message is bigger than “do not be friends with naughty children”

The goal is not to make children afraid of everyone who behaves differently. It is to help them recognise, evaluate, and choose healthy influences—while adults remain attentive to the environments children experience every day.

Social influence can be positive or negative

Frequently observed environmentBehaviours that may be learned
People who are disciplined and responsibleConsistency, punctuality, accountability
People who routinely lieLying may begin to look like an ordinary way to avoid consequences
A group that treats others with respectHealthy communication and empathy
A group that constantly humiliates othersInsults may be reframed as humour or normal conversation

Conclusion

We often focus on what we teach people directly, but overlook what we allow them to absorb from their everyday environment.

Sometimes people do not change because someone gives them advice. They change because they spend so much time in an environment where a particular behaviour is treated as normal.

“Be careful about the environment you expose your children to. You do not have to teach them certain behaviours. Sometimes, repeated exposure and observation are enough for those behaviours to start feeling normal.”

This does not mean isolating children from people who are different. It means recognising that the social environment is one of the powerful informal educators in human life.
