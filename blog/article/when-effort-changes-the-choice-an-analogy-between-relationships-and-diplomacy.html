When Effort Changes the Choice: An Analogy Between Relationships and Diplomacy

Angga Conni Saputra
•
October 1, 2026

Consider this simple situation. You have two options, A and B. They are equally attractive. Then A hurts you, while B does not. Later, both approach you. All else being equal, why would you not prefer B?

Now change one variable: A makes a serious effort to repair the damage. A shows up, changes behaviour, sacrifices time, accepts a commitment that was previously resisted, and consistently demonstrates that the relationship matters. Suddenly, A is no longer the same option you evaluated at the moment of the original injury.

The intuition is not really about beauty. It is about accumulated value.

Two people can begin with similar baseline value. Their subsequent choices change the history of the relationship. One person may create trust, the other may create damage. One may invest effort, the other may remain passive. The eventual choice is therefore not based only on what each person was at the beginning, but also on what interacting with each person has become.

That is the analogy I want to explore here. It is useful in relationships, but it can also be used as a way of thinking about diplomacy, negotiation and institutional relationships.

1. The relationship thought experiment

Start with the cleanest case.

A and B are equally attractive. A hurts you. B does not. A and B both later express interest.

A = baseline 8 − damage 3 = 5 B = baseline 8 + stable history 0 = 8

In this simplified model, B has an advantage. Not because B became more beautiful, richer or more impressive, but because the interaction history is different.

Now suppose A begins to invest.

A = baseline 8 − damage 3 + repair effort 4 = 9 B = baseline 8 = 8

Now A can become competitive again.

That does not mean that every hurt can be repaired by enough effort. Some actions change the decision boundary permanently. Some people will not want to continue even after a sincere apology. Consent, boundaries and personal values remain decisive. The calculation is therefore a thought experiment: it shows why a choice can change when the inputs change.

Effort does not erase history. It changes what the history means for the next decision.

2. “A + effort” is not the same as “A says sorry”

This is where the idea becomes more interesting.

Effort has levels. It is not a binary variable where someone either “tries” or “doesn't try.”

LevelRelationship exampleWhat it signals
Presencekeeping company, replying, showing upattention

Practical sacrificehelping with something inconvenient or difficultinvestment

This is why a person can say, “I already made an effort,” while the other person still experiences no meaningful repair. The two people may be assigning different values to the same action.

For example, spending ten minutes sending a message may be effort. Changing a long-term plan can also be effort. They are both effort, but their opportunity costs are different.

3. The commitment example

Consider a very specific example: someone initially does not want marriage, while the other person wants marriage. Later, the first person decides to marry because the relationship itself has become sufficiently valuable to justify a major change of plan.

That decision is more than a romantic sentence. It is a costly commitment.

In the model, the relevant point is not “marriage is always the correct repair.” It is that changing a major prior decision carries a larger opportunity cost than simply saying, “I am sorry.”

Important distinction: effort should never be treated as entitlement. A large sacrifice does not create ownership over another person. It is simply evidence about what someone is willing to invest.

4. The surprising case: B is less attractive, but B invests

Now make the baseline values different.

A is very attractive: baseline 9.

B is more ordinary: baseline 6.

Yet B provides meaningful effort, consistency and trust.

A = 9 + 0 = 9 B = 6 + 3 = 9

The two options can become comparable.

This is a useful reminder that people do not necessarily optimise a single attribute. Physical attractiveness can be important, but so can reliability, treatment, compatibility, trust, shared goals and the cost of maintaining the relationship.

Now reverse the situation.

B has the lower baseline, then hurts you, then provides no repair effort.

A = 9 + effort 2 = 11 B = 6 − damage 3 + effort 0 = 3

In the simplified model, there is no reason to assume B remains competitive merely because B was previously chosen or previously available.

The option with the highest starting value does not always win. But a lower starting value also does not automatically disappear. What matters is the accumulated position after interaction, effort, damage and cost are considered.

5. The same logic appears in diplomacy

Now translate the thought experiment into international relations.

Imagine a state says something damaging about another state. Perhaps the statement is inaccurate, provocative, careless or simply misunderstood. Regardless of intent, the other side can now incorporate that event into its assessment of the relationship.

The analogy is not that governments literally “choose the prettier person.” The analogy is that relationships accumulate history.

A diplomatic partner may be evaluated on:

Strategic value
What does cooperation provide?
Trust
Can commitments be relied upon?
Friction
How difficult is cooperation?
Reputation
What signal does this behaviour send?
Reciprocity
Does cooperation tend to be returned?
Risk
What additional uncertainty is created?

That makes a diplomatic blunder conceptually similar to damage in a relationship.

Net Diplomatic Position = Interest + Trust + Cooperation Value + Repair Effort − Damage − Friction − Risk

6. The Suez Canal thought experiment — and the important caveat

Suppose, purely as a hypothetical example, State A and State B both seek the same type of access or cooperation. State A has just created a diplomatic problem with another country by making an inaccurate or sensitive statement. State B has not.

The intuitive question is: “Would the damaged relationship create extra friction?”

Possibly—but the actual Suez Canal case cannot be reduced to personal preference. The Suez Canal Authority states that transit is available to vessels of all nations subject to the rules of navigation, and the Constantinople Convention establishes a principle of free use of the canal under its terms. The SCA also states that it should not discriminate between comparable users in conflict with those rules. citeturn141755search1turn141755search0turn141755search4

So the diplomatic analogy should be read this way:

A damaged bilateral relationship may create political or diplomatic friction around a request, but it does not replace the legal and institutional rules governing an international transit system.

That distinction is crucial. The model is about relationship dynamics, not a claim that diplomatic feelings can simply override law or operating rules.

7. The broker scenario: “Let me help bring you closer”

There is another pattern.

Someone says:

“Come on. I can help connect you with them. Let's cooperate.”

The other side agrees. The broker begins creating access, trust and coordination.

Then the broker makes a careless statement.

Maybe it is intentional. Maybe accidental. Maybe the wording is simply misunderstood.

Now the broker's value proposition has changed.

Broker Value = Access Created + Trust Built − Miscommunication − Repair Cost

This is why diplomatic relationships often require more than saying, “That was not what I meant.” The broker or intermediary may need clarification, correction, reassurance, a new proposal, additional transparency or a concrete action that restores confidence.

In other words, the worse the damage, the more credible the repair usually has to be.

How to read the simulation

Move one slider at a time. Notice that a lower-baseline option can become competitive when effort and trust rise. Also notice the reverse: a high-baseline option can lose its advantage when damage, friction or ongoing cost become large.

The model uses:

Final Score = Baseline + Effort + Trust − Damage − Cost

For the diplomacy mode, interpret “baseline” as strategic or cooperation value, “trust” as relationship capital, “effort” as confidence-building or repair, “damage” as diplomatic friction/reputational cost, and “cost” as operational or political risk.

9. What this model explains — and what it does not

It explains trade-offs.
A lower starting value can be offset by meaningful effort under some assumptions.
It explains repair.
Damage changes the starting point for the next decision; repair changes that position again.
It explains why history matters.
Two otherwise similar options may not remain equivalent after different interactions.
It does not predict people.
Humans can reject an option despite a high score because values, boundaries and emotions are not fully captured here.

10. The deeper analogy

The deeper idea is not “beauty versus effort.” It is relative value under relationship history.

Imagine two suppliers. One has excellent technical capability but repeatedly creates friction. Another is slightly less capable but reliable, responsive and cooperative. A procurement decision may look different once transaction costs and reliability are included.

Imagine two organizations seeking a partnership. One has strong resources but has recently created avoidable trust problems. The other has fewer resources but a stronger history of cooperation. Again, the decision is not only about the initial asset base.

Imagine a diplomatic intermediary who promises to make cooperation easier. The intermediary can increase value by creating access and confidence. But a careless statement can destroy part of that value and trigger repair costs.

Across all three cases, the same structure appears:

Starting Value → Interaction → Trust / Damage → Effort / Repair → Revised Value → New Choice

The sequence is dynamic. That is the important part.

11. The idea of “A + effort” in one sentence

A person, organization or state can become a different option in the decision-maker's eyes because the relationship history has changed—not because its original characteristics suddenly changed.

That is why “A was better on paper” and “B was chosen” are not necessarily contradictory.

The paper may describe the baseline.

The relationship describes what happened after the baseline.

12. Closing: value is not static

So perhaps the real lesson is this:

When two options begin with comparable value, the way they treat you can become part of the value calculation.

Effort can add value.

Trust can add value.

Damage can subtract value.

Repair can restore part of what was lost.

And large commitments can matter precisely because they carry opportunity costs.

This is also why the analogy with diplomacy is useful—but only up to the point where legal rules, institutions, strategic interests and other constraints take over. In relationships, no formula can override consent. In diplomacy, no informal “relationship score” overrides treaty obligations or formal navigation rules.

The useful question is therefore not “Who is objectively better?” It is: “Given the history, the costs, the effort and the constraints, how has the relative value of these options changed?”

This article is a conceptual framework and simulation developed to make the intuition visible. The scores are illustrative, not empirically calibrated measures of human attraction, trust, or state behaviour.
