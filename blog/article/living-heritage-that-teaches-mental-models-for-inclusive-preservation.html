Living Heritage & Mental Models

Living Heritage That Teaches Mental Models for Inclusive Preservation

Angga Conni Saputra
•
September 21, 2026

What if living heritage preserves not only what communities do, but also how communities learn to think?

When we talk about Intangible Cultural Heritage (ICH), we often talk about dances, songs, crafts, food, rituals, languages and ceremonies. But living heritage can also carry relational habits, ethical concepts and ways of making sense of other people. This article explores that idea through husnuzan, tepa selira, and a practical mental model for divergent and convergent reasoning.

Core mental model
DIVERGE → INVESTIGATE → CONVERGE → CLOSE

Divergence expands the hypothesis space. Investigation reduces uncertainty. Convergence closes the model when the evidence is sufficient.

When Observation Becomes Conclusion

Consider a simple observation: “She still chats with her ex.”

A fast convergent interpretation might be: “She has not moved on.” It is efficient, but it may also be premature. A divergent approach asks: “Why?”

Premature convergence
Chatting with an ex → not over the ex.
Divergent first
Chatting with an ex → why?
Maybe unresolved feelings. Maybe family ties. Maybe work. Maybe practical dependence. Maybe friendship. Maybe a hidden variable.

Ask the person. The explanation might be: “I am already over them. Our families are still connected, and sometimes the ex is simply the person who connects us to someone we need.”

Now the model changes. The observation was accurate. The first explanation was not sufficiently tested.

This is the distinction I care about: an observation is not automatically a causal explanation.

Why Divergent Thinking Can Be Slow When Knowledge Is Zero

If your knowledge about a subject is close to zero, divergence can become expensive. A convergent mind may find one plausible explanation and stop. A divergent mind may generate five, ten, or twenty possibilities without knowing which variables actually matter.

So divergence is not the final destination. It is the expansion phase.

The learning loop becomes:

  1. Observe: record what actually happened.
  2. Diverge: generate plausible explanations.
  3. Investigate: identify information that can distinguish them.
  4. Converge: choose the explanation best supported by evidence.
  5. Close: treat it as a working model, not eternal truth.

This is why I do not see divergent and convergent thinking as competing personalities. They are better understood as complementary phases of reasoning.

Husnuzan as a Cognitive Pause

Husnuzan is commonly understood in Islamic ethics as maintaining a good or positive assumption rather than immediately jumping to suspicion. In this article, I am not treating it as proof that another person is good. I am treating it as a useful epistemic pause: do not automatically select the most negative explanation when information is incomplete.

Instead of:

“He did not reply because he does not respect me.”

start with:

“He did not reply.”

Then ask: What else could explain this?

Maybe he is busy. Maybe he forgot. Maybe he did not see it. Maybe the message was unclear. Maybe he disagrees. Maybe he intentionally ignored it. Investigation comes before judgment.

Tepa Selira: Add the Other Person's Context

Tepa selira is often associated with considering another person's position, feelings and context. That creates another powerful reasoning move: “What information might they have that I do not have?”

The same behaviour can mean different things inside different contexts. A silence may be reflection, fatigue, discomfort, self-control or anger. A direct statement may be honesty to one person and aggression to another.

Tepa selira does not require us to declare every interpretation equally valid. It asks us to remember that our own context is not the entire system.

Living Heritage as Cognitive Infrastructure

This lens can also be applied to other living-heritage practices. UNESCO's Indonesian elements include Pantun, Angklung, Traditions of Pencak Silat, Jamu wellness culture, Wayang and others. The following are interpretive readings, not claims that UNESCO formally defines these elements as divergent-thinking methods.

Pantun

Can illustrate indirect, relational communication: the message and the relationship are considered together.

Angklung

Can illustrate distributed intelligence: individual parts become meaningful through coordination.

Pencak Silat

Can illustrate capability with restraint: being able to act is not identical to needing to act.

Jamu

Can illustrate context sensitivity: the same label does not necessarily imply the same response for every person.

Wayang

Can illustrate perspective expansion: complex social situations can be explored through multiple characters and viewpoints.

The Inclusion Connection

Premature closure can quietly produce exclusion. We see one behaviour, assign one label, and stop investigating:

“Lazy.” “Difficult.” “Arrogant.” “Does not care.” “Not interested.”

Divergent reasoning inserts a small but powerful pause:

“What else could be happening?”

Maybe there is a missing resource. Maybe instructions were interpreted differently. Maybe cultural expectations differ. Maybe a constraint is invisible to us. Maybe the behaviour makes sense inside the person's information system.

This does not mean every alternative explanation is correct. It means we do not know yet.

How to Train: Divergent First, Convergent Later

Exercise 1 — Separate observation from interpretation. Write one sentence describing only what you observed. Then write the story your brain immediately created. Keep them separate.

Exercise 2 — 5 What-Else Rule. Every time you form a conclusion, generate at least five plausible alternatives before deciding.

Exercise 3 — Find the discriminating variable. Ask: “What information would distinguish these hypotheses?” This question turns endless brainstorming into investigation.

Exercise 4 — Converge explicitly. Write: “Given the evidence currently available, my working explanation is…” This forces the convergence step instead of letting it happen invisibly.

Exercise 5 — Reopen. Write one piece of new evidence that would make you reconsider your model. This prevents convergence from becoming rigidity.

Mini Practice Case

Observation: “My colleague did not respond to my message.”

  1. List five possible explanations.
  2. Which explanation is your brain selecting first?
  3. What missing information could distinguish them?
  4. What would you ask or observe?
  5. After investigating, write your working model.

Knowledge = 0: How to Learn Without Getting Lost

When knowledge is near zero, start with basic structure before broad divergence. Learn the vocabulary, main components and obvious relationships. Then open the hypothesis space.

A practical sequence is:

BUILD BASE
basic vocabulary
DIVERGE
alternatives
INVESTIGATE
evidence
CONVERGE
best-supported model
CLOSE
working model

The skill is not to remain divergent forever. The skill is to know when to stop expanding and start testing.

A Simple Mental Checklist

Before concluding:
  • What did I actually observe?
  • What am I assuming?
  • What else could explain it?
Before closing:
  • What evidence supports this?
  • What evidence would change my mind?
  • Have I confused a pattern with a cause?

Perhaps this is one of the quietest connections between living heritage and cognitive practice: cultural concepts can give us vocabulary for behaviours that are useful far beyond their original setting.

Diverge before you judge.
Investigate before you conclude.
Converge when the evidence is sufficient.
Reopen when reality gives you new information.

Living heritage as a living skill
Maybe preservation is not only about keeping a tradition alive.
Maybe it is also about keeping the social intelligence inside that tradition usable for the next generation.

Interactive learning idea

Use the mental-model diagram above as the visual anchor, then turn each stage into a short scenario card in the article: the reader identifies the observation, generates alternative hypotheses, chooses the missing variable, and only then selects a working explanation.

Concept note

This article treats Husnuzan and Tepa Selira as cultural/ethical concepts and interprets them as possible cognitive practices. It does not claim that UNESCO formally defines them as divergent-thinking techniques, nor that the selected Indonesian ICH elements are officially mental-model frameworks. Interpretations should remain open to community context and plurality.
