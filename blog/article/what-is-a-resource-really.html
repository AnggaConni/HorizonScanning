WHAT IS A RESOURCE, REALLY?

The Politics of Gratitude: When Loyalty Becomes More Valuable Than Expertise

When people hear the word resources, they often think of money, oil, minerals, land, or other natural resources. But there is another resource without which most organizations cannot turn budgets, infrastructure, or plans into results: human capability.

People do not simply occupy positions. They interpret evidence, solve problems, build systems, maintain institutional memory, make decisions, and deliver services. Human beings are not interchangeable line items. Their knowledge, judgement, experience, and ability to learn are part of an organization’s real capacity.

And yet, the way some organizations allocate money can reveal a strange contradiction: they may spend generously to preserve political relationships, while treating professional expertise as an expense to be minimized.

1. The contradiction: expensive connections, discounted expertise

Consider a pattern—not a claim about every organization, country, or political appointment:

  1. An organization is willing to spend substantial money on a person connected to a powerful official, political ally, family, or inner circle.
  2. The appointment may preserve access, reward loyalty, fulfill an obligation, or strengthen a relationship.
  3. When the organization needs a specialist whose work is genuinely important, the hiring conversation suddenly becomes about finding the lowest possible price.
  4. The specialist’s expertise is described as “support,” “nice to have,” or replaceable—even when the organization knows the role is critical.
  5. The decision is shaped less by the value and risk of the work than by who should receive the opportunity or whose relationship must be protected.

This is the paradox: the organization may understand the value of expertise, yet its incentives reward something else. It is not necessarily that leaders cannot recognize skill. Sometimes skill simply does not deliver the same immediate political or relational return as patronage.

A necessary distinction: a low offer alone does not prove favoritism. Pay can be constrained by a salary scale, procurement rules, budget limits, or a poorly designed job description. The concern is the pattern: discretionary spending for connections alongside persistent underinvestment in capabilities the organization says it needs.

2. What forces can push an organization toward this?

Patronage is not always one person making one obviously corrupt decision. It can emerge from a chain of pressures. A politician may need allies; an executive may need political cover; a manager may fear retaliation; a hiring process may be opaque; and the costs of poor technical decisions may be delayed or borne by someone else.

Conceptual systems map—not a claim that every appointment follows this exact sequence. Multiple pressures can reinforce one another.

Several well-established concepts help describe these dynamics:

  • Political patronage: positions or benefits are distributed through political relationships rather than solely through role-related merit.
  • Clientelism: a reciprocal exchange in which a benefit is made conditional on political support or loyalty.
  • Nepotism: favoritism toward relatives.
  • Cronyism: favoritism toward friends or close associates.
  • Organizational capture: decisions are bent toward a narrow group’s interests rather than the organization’s stated purpose.

These terms overlap, but they are not identical. A political appointment is not automatically clientelism; the key issue is whether benefits and loyalty are exchanged, whether merit is displaced, and whether the process is accountable.

3. Why can an ordinary appointment be valued more than an expert?

One answer is that organizations can be operating with two different “value systems” at once.

Relational benefits can be immediate and visible: someone is pleased, an alliance is maintained, a promise is fulfilled. The value of expertise may be less visible at first. A specialist can prevent a failure that never happens, improve a decision that would otherwise be poor, or build a system whose benefits arrive months later.

This creates a familiar organizational trap: the cost of hiring an expert is visible today; the cost of not having expertise may appear much later. When decision-makers are rewarded for short-term relationships and not held accountable for long-term performance, the incentives can become badly misaligned.

It is also possible to undervalue expertise because it is difficult to assess. If leaders do not know how to distinguish high-quality technical work from a polished presentation, they may fall back on price, familiarity, credentials, or personal trust. That is a management problem—not proof that expertise has no value.

4. The research: this is not merely a personal impression

Research documents that political connections can affect who is hired and how public organizations select employees. For example, Colonnelli, Prem, and Teso’s study of Brazil’s public workforce from 1997 to 2014 found that political connections were an important determinant of public employment, that patronage played a role, and that political considerations led to the selection of less competent individuals in the setting they studied.

That finding should be interpreted carefully: it is evidence from a specific country, period, and research design—not a universal claim about every political appointment. Still, it illustrates the risk when political connections substitute for competence.

OECD guidance on public integrity emphasizes merit-based, transparent recruitment and objective qualification and performance criteria. It also notes that patronage and nepotism can undermine integrity and, in extreme cases, create roles to reward allies rather than meet real organizational needs.

Meanwhile, the OECD’s guidance on senior public service recruitment recognizes a genuine tension: political responsiveness matters, but appointments also need leadership assessment, transparency, accountability, and safeguards against undue political influence.

5. If we cannot reject the appointee, what should we do?

Here is the practical question. Suppose someone has already been appointed. Perhaps the appointment was political. Perhaps rejecting the person is not within your authority—or is simply not feasible.

Do we give up? Do we silently accept that they do not understand the role?

I would argue for a different response: do not confuse accepting the appointment with accepting incompetence. If the person holds the responsibility, they must learn the responsibility.

That does not mean humiliating them, shouting at them, or pretending that every gap can be solved through pressure. It means building a disciplined learning and accountability process. “Pecut” should mean a demanding standard, structured practice, direct feedback, and consequences for refusing to learn—not abuse.

The goal is capability, not punishment. The assessment should match the actual duties and be fair, documented, and proportionate.

Step 1 — Make the job requirements explicit

Do not rely on vague expectations such as “be a good leader” or “understand the sector.” Translate the role into observable capabilities: what must this person know, explain, decide, produce, and be accountable for?

Step 2 — Diagnose before prescribing

Ask the person to explain the core concepts, walk through a realistic case, or review a sample deliverable. A knowledge gap, a lack of experience, a language barrier, and a refusal to learn are different problems. They require different responses.

Step 3 — Create a learning contract

Agree on a mentor, learning materials, practice assignments, deadlines, and the evidence that will demonstrate progress. A title should not exempt someone from learning; it should make the learning expectations clearer.

Step 4 — Coach, practise, and challenge

Use real work. Ask the appointee to defend a decision, explain the evidence, identify uncertainty, and respond to feedback. Repeat the cycle. A demanding leader does not merely say “you should know this.” They create the conditions and insist that the person reaches the required standard.

Step 5 — Assess performance, not loyalty or confidence

Someone may sound confident and still misunderstand the subject. Someone may be inexperienced and learn quickly. Use work samples, scenario-based assessments, transparent criteria, and feedback from relevant experts.

Step 6 — Keep accountability real

If the person meets the standard, give them greater autonomy. If they are improving but not there yet, provide additional support and time where appropriate. If they repeatedly refuse to learn or cannot safely perform essential duties, restrict decision authority, redesign the role, or pursue reassignment through fair and lawful procedures.

My principle: if we cannot always control who gets appointed, we must at least insist on what the appointment requires. Political selection cannot be a permanent exemption from professional standards.

6. Expertise is not a luxury item

Imagine an organization working in science but failing to understand scientific evidence. Imagine a public health leader who cannot distinguish a strong study from a weak one. Imagine a monitoring and evaluation manager who cannot explain indicators, data quality, or the limits of causal claims.

Should we be embarrassed? Yes—not because people begin their careers knowing everything, but because a position carries a duty to learn what is necessary to do the job responsibly.

There is a difference between “I do not know yet” and “I do not need to know.” The first can be the beginning of professional growth. The second can become a risk to the entire organization.

And the same standard should apply to how we pay people. If expertise is essential, compensation should reflect the required level of skill, responsibility, scarcity, and impact—within transparent and defensible pay structures. Paying the lowest possible amount is not automatically efficient if it increases delivery risk, turnover, rework, or the likelihood of failure.

7. Human resources means developing human capability

We cannot claim to value people while treating their knowledge as disposable. Nor can we claim to value expertise while refusing to invest in it.

A healthy organization should be able to answer:

  • What capabilities do we actually need?
  • Are people selected and paid using clear, role-related criteria?
  • Can we explain why a position exists and what value it creates?
  • Do appointees receive support to learn—and are they held accountable for doing so?
  • Can professional experts speak honestly when evidence contradicts political preference?

These are not anti-politics questions. They are questions about whether an organization can remain competent, trustworthy, and useful while navigating political realities.

Being appointed is not the same as being competent. Being connected is not the same as being qualified. And being grateful to someone who gave you an opportunity does not exempt you from the responsibility to do your job properly.

The appointment may be political. The responsibility is still professional.

That is what we lack—not necessarily resources or money, but a consistent commitment to developing human capability.

So before asking how much an organization should spend on its resources, perhaps we should ask a more fundamental question:

Do we even understand what a resource is?
