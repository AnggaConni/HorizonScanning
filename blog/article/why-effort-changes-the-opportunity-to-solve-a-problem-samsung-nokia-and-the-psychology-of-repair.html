Why Effort Changes the Opportunity to Solve a Problem: Samsung, Nokia, and the Psychology of Repair

Angga Conni Saputra
•
October 29, 2026

Psychology • Strategy • Conflict Repair • Systems Thinking

Effort does not guarantee success. But relevant, sustained effort can change what is possible: it creates more opportunities to understand a problem, test solutions, reduce harm, and rebuild trust.

When a conflict remains unresolved, people sometimes say, “If it is meant to be, things will work out,” or “Time will heal everything.” Those statements may offer comfort, but they can also hide an important question: what is anyone actually doing to make a better outcome possible?

Problems do not all require the same response. A small misunderstanding may need one honest conversation. A serious breach of trust may require accountability, repeated evidence of change, and time. The size and nature of the damage should shape the kind of effort used to repair it.

Effort matters not because suffering proves sincerity, but because meaningful action can create opportunities that passive waiting leaves unused.

1. Effort changes the opportunity—not the guarantee

Imagine a conflict as a situation with several possible paths. One path is avoidance: do nothing, hope emotions fade, and assume the other person will eventually move on. Another is repair: clarify what happened, listen to the impact, accept responsibility, propose a relevant remedy, and follow through.

Repair does not guarantee reconciliation. The other person may still decline to continue the relationship, and that choice must be respected. Yet active repair can create opportunities that did not exist before: an opportunity to correct misinformation, understand the harm, demonstrate accountability, or make an informed decision about the future.

Low or misdirected effortLeaves important questions unanswered, relies on assumptions, and may allow avoidable damage to continue.
Relevant, sustained effortCreates more chances to learn what went wrong, test a remedy, respond to feedback, and show that change is real.
OutcomeStill depends on many factors: the severity of harm, timing, context, the quality of the response, and whether both people want to participate.

3. Case study: Samsung Galaxy Note7—accepting a costly remedy

In 2016, Samsung's Galaxy Note7 phones were found to have a battery-related fire hazard. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission's recall notice described risks of overheating and catching fire, and offered consumers a refund or replacement remedy. Samsung announced that it had stopped sales and would voluntarily replace devices, then worked with regulators and retail partners on the recall process.

In the United States, Samsung's exchange program offered customers choices that included exchanging the Note7 for a Galaxy S7 or S7 edge, with a refund of the price difference, or taking part in the replacement process. When replacement devices also presented safety concerns, Samsung stopped production and sales and expanded the recall to include both original and replacement devices.

That is a useful example of costly corrective action. A company could not responsibly solve a safety problem with a reassuring slogan alone. It needed to stop sales, coordinate logistics, give customers a practical remedy, investigate the failure, and accept commercial consequences.

What the example shows: the company created a more credible path to consumer protection by taking concrete action, not merely by saying that customer safety mattered.

Samsung's response was not flawless, and the replacement-device problem showed that the first corrective action was insufficient. This makes the case more instructive, not less: effort must include learning and adjustment. A costly first response is not enough if evidence shows that the underlying risk remains.

Samsung's 2017 sustainability report later described extensive investigation, including testing more than 200,000 devices and 30,000 batteries, and said it had worked to identify the causes of the battery failures. These figures are reported by Samsung itself, so they should be understood as the company's account of its investigation.

The lesson is not that every organisation should always choose the most expensive response. The lesson is that when the potential harm is serious, a proportionate response may require accepting substantial cost to protect people and address the damage.

4. The contrasting case: Nokia and the cost of a delayed strategic response

Nokia is often described as a company that “ignored customers” or simply refused to innovate. That is too simplistic. Its decline in the smartphone market had multiple causes, including organisational conflict, strategic decisions, a fragmented software environment, and the industry's shift from hardware-led competition toward operating systems, apps, and platform ecosystems.

Research discussed by INSEAD notes that Nokia was initially dismissive of the iPhone and Android challenge while its Symbian operating system was ageing and its app ecosystem struggled to match competitors. A separate study of Nokia's decline describes how organisational design and strategic choices made it harder for the company to respond effectively to the transition.

Nokia did try to respond, including through its Windows Phone partnership with Microsoft. But effort is not automatically effective just because an organisation is busy. If a response arrives late, relies on risky assumptions, or does not meet what users and developers need, it may fail to recover the opportunity that has already shifted elsewhere.

Important correction: Nokia did not simply “go bankrupt.” Its handset business was sold to Microsoft; Nokia continued as a company and focused more heavily on network infrastructure and other businesses. The more accurate lesson is that it lost its former dominance in mobile phones, not that the entire company disappeared.

This contrasts with the Note7 recall in a useful way. Samsung's safety crisis demanded immediate corrective action to reduce direct harm. Nokia's platform crisis demanded earlier strategic adaptation, stronger software execution, and a more effective response to a changing ecosystem. Different problems require different kinds of effort.

5. Why effort can change the odds: five mechanisms

It improves diagnosis.
Effort spent listening, investigating, and checking evidence reduces the chance of solving the wrong problem.
It creates options.
People who explore alternatives can discover routes that passive waiting never reveals.
It reduces ongoing harm.
In safety or trust crises, timely action can limit further damage while a fuller solution is developed.
It creates observable evidence.
Promises describe intentions. Actions and follow-through give others evidence to evaluate.
It enables adaptation.
When the first attempt fails, feedback and continued work make correction possible.

These mechanisms explain why effort can improve the probability of an effective solution. They do not mean that effort alone controls outcomes. A well-executed plan can still fail because of external constraints, insufficient resources, incompatible goals, or damage that cannot be reversed.

6. Effort must match the damage—and the actual problem

Consider three examples:

A misunderstandingRelevant effort might mean clarifying what was said, listening without interruption, and correcting a false assumption.
A breach of trustRelevant effort might mean admitting what happened, answering reasonable questions, changing the behavior, and remaining consistent over time.
A product safety failureRelevant effort may require stopping sales, recalling the product, providing refunds or replacements, investigating the cause, and preventing recurrence.

Notice that the amount of effort is not the only issue. Its direction matters. An expensive gift may do little to repair a repeated breach of trust. A public apology may do little to protect consumers if the dangerous product remains in circulation.

The right question is not simply “How much effort was made?” It is “Was the effort proportionate to the harm, directed at the real cause, and sustained long enough to make a difference?”

7. A practical framework for evaluating effort

Use these questions when assessing a conflict, a project failure, a customer complaint, or an organisational crisis:

What exactly was damaged?
Identify the harm, its scale, who experienced it, and whether it is continuing.
Who has responsibility to act?
Separate the responsibility to repair harm from the separate question of whether the affected person chooses to forgive or continue.
What action would address the cause?
Choose a remedy that fits the actual problem rather than a symbolic gesture that merely looks impressive.
What obstacles are real?
Account for safety, resources, time, access, and other constraints. Look for reasonable alternatives rather than demanding reckless sacrifice.
What evidence would show progress?
Define observable signs: a corrected process, a kept commitment, fewer repeat incidents, clearer communication, or improved user outcomes.
What happens if the first attempt fails?
Review the evidence, acknowledge shortcomings, and adapt the response. Do not assume that good intentions make an ineffective remedy sufficient.

8. In relationships, effort is not a test of suffering

There is a risk in talking about effort: people may interpret it as a demand to prove love through dramatic gestures, unlimited availability, or unsafe sacrifices. That is not the point.

Showing up uninvited, lying to provoke a reaction, manufacturing an emergency, or ignoring someone's boundaries is not a healthy way to measure care. These behaviours can create pressure and confusion rather than genuine repair.

More useful evidence includes whether someone takes initiative, communicates honestly, makes realistic plans, follows through, accepts responsibility, and changes the behaviour that caused the problem. If circumstances prevent a planned action, proposing a reasonable alternative can also show commitment.

And the person who was hurt retains the right to say no. Effort can create an opportunity for repair; it does not create an entitlement to forgiveness, access, or reconciliation.

Conclusion: Effort changes what becomes possible

The Samsung Note7 case illustrates how a company can respond to a serious product safety problem with costly corrective action. Nokia's smartphone decline illustrates a different risk: when a changing environment demands a timely and effective strategic response, fragmented or poorly aligned decisions can allow an opportunity to disappear. Neither case is a simple morality tale, and neither proves that effort guarantees success.

Together, they point to a broader principle. Effective effort creates opportunities to diagnose, act, learn, and adapt. Insufficient or misdirected effort can leave problems unresolved or allow them to grow. The key is not effort for its own sake, but effort that matches the scale and nature of the problem.

Time does not recall a product. Good intentions do not fix a broken process. And wanting a relationship to heal does not, by itself, rebuild trust. Meaningful effort is the bridge between recognising a problem and creating a real opportunity to solve it.

This article uses business cases as illustrations of a general problem-solving principle. Corporate outcomes have multiple causes; the cases should not be interpreted as proof that effort alone caused success or failure.
